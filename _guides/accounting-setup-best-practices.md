Most small organizations don't fail at bookkeeping because their treasurer
was careless. They fail because the books were set up without a plan, and
fixing them later costs ten times the effort. This guide walks through a
setup that survives audits, board meetings and volunteer turnover.

## Separate your money first

Open a dedicated bank account in the organization's name before anything
else. Commingling personal and organizational funds is the single fastest
way to lose grant eligibility, board trust and your 501(c)(3) status.

- One operating account is enough to start; add a savings account only when
  you actually hold restricted funds.
- Use a debit card, never a personal credit card, for organizational
  spending - and reimburse volunteers with documentation, never cash.
- Order a duplicate statement to go to a second person (see [controls](#build-basic-controls)).

## Build a simple chart of accounts

A chart of accounts is just the list of categories your money flows
through. Resist the urge to make it detailed - ten income accounts and
twelve expense accounts is plenty for most community organizations.

| Type | Keep it simple | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Income | 3–5 accounts | Donations, Grants, Program fees, Fundraisers |
| Expense | 8–12 accounts | Rent, Supplies, Program costs, Events, Payroll |

Two conventions pay off for years:

1. **Separate program costs from admin costs.** Funders ask for this split
   constantly, and retrofitting it is painful.
2. **Name accounts the way your board talks.** "Youth program supplies"
   beats "4100-15 indirect program expenditure."

> If you expect to receive restricted grants (funds earmarked for a specific
> purpose), set up fund or class tracking on day one. Retrofitting is
> painful. In QuickBooks this is **Classes**; in Wave it's limited, so plan
> accordingly.

## Record everything, reconcile monthly

Reconciliation is the habit that makes every other practice possible. Pick
a fixed day each month - the 15th works well - and:

1. Match every bank transaction to a receipt or invoice.
2. Confirm your book balance matches the bank statement exactly.
3. Investigate every discrepancy the same day, not "eventually."

Store receipts digitally from the start: a shared cloud folder organized by
month, photographed on the day of purchase, is enough. The IRS accepts
electronic records; a shoebox does not scale.

## Build basic controls

Small teams can't run corporate segregation of duties, but three cheap
controls prevent nearly all common fraud:

- **Two-person rule.** Whoever writes the checks never reconciles the
  books. Even with two volunteers, this one split is achievable.
- **Board review.** A one-page income and expense report presented at every
  board meeting, signed off in the minutes.
- **Statement delivery.** Have the bank send a duplicate statement to a
  second officer or board member.

## Choose software that fits your stage

Don't buy more than you need:

- **Just starting (under ~$10k/yr):** Wave (free) or a well-maintained
  spreadsheet template.
- **Growing (~$10k–$100k/yr):** QuickBooks Simple Start or Premier
  Nonprofit via [TechSoup](https://www.techsoup.org) for fund tracking.
- **Grant-funded, payroll, 990 filings:** QuickBooks Premier Nonprofit or
  an accountant-backed system like Aplos or Sage Intacct.

## When to get professional help

Bring in a CPA when you: take your first restricted grant, hire your first
employee, or approach your first annual filing (Form 990 in the US). A few
hours of professional review each year is far cheaper than reconstructing
three years of books - and it's exactly what our
[accounting workshops](mailto:contact@goodsoftware.foundation) can help you
prepare for.
